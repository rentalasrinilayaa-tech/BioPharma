How to read drug-development data

Five minutes of background that makes every page on this site — and every pharma headline you read — much easier to understand.

The four phases of a clinical trial

A new drug must climb a ladder of human studies before approval. Each step asks a bigger, more expensive question — and most drugs fall off along the way. Roughly one in ten drugs that enter Phase 1 eventually reaches approval.

Phase 1 Is it safe? Small groups (20–100 people, often healthy volunteers) test dosing and watch for harmful effects.

Phase 2 Does it work? Hundreds of patients with the disease. First real evidence of effectiveness — and where most drugs fail.

Phase 3 Is it better? Hundreds to thousands of patients, usually compared against the current standard treatment or a placebo. Success here is the basis for approval.

Phase 4 What happens at scale? Studies after approval, monitoring safety in the real world across many more patients and years.

What's an NCT number?

Every registered trial gets a permanent ID like NCT04368728 — "NCT" for National Clinical Trial plus eight digits. It's the trial's fingerprint: paste it into ClinicalTrials.gov and you'll find the official record, including who can join, where the sites are, and results when published. Every card on our Pipeline Tracker links to its NCT record.

Adverse events ≠ side effects

Our Drug Explorer shows adverse-event reports from the FDA's FAERS system — reports filed by patients, pharmacists and doctors after someone taking a drug experienced something bad. Crucially, a report does not prove the drug caused the event: people taking heart medication also have heart problems, with or without the drug. High report counts can also simply mean a drug is very widely used. Treat the data as a map of what's being reported, not a verdict — the drug's official label lists its confirmed side effects.

Why "approved in the US" isn't "approved everywhere"

Each region has its own regulator: the FDA in the United States, the EMA in the European Union, the MHRA in the UK, the PMDA in Japan, the CDSCO in India, and so on. A drug can be approved in one country years before another, or never. Our approvals feed covers the US FDA, whose data is the most open; trial-location data on the Drug Explorer shows where a drug is being tested worldwide.
